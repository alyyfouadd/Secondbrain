# Slogans and Song — Alex Foods

**Brand Foundation deliverable 4 of 8** for [[Alex Foods]]: brand slogans plus the giveaway song, written and ready for posts, ads and campaign material. This note is the method and the working file. Build sequence in [[Foundation Roadmap]], brand facts in [[Alex Foods Brands]].

---

## 1. The correction that has to come first

[[Marketing]] is the craft reference for every piece of copy TSA writes, and most of it carries here unchanged. **But a slogan is not a headline, and applying the headline rules literally will produce four unusable lines.**

A headline sells one click, once, and gets replaced tomorrow. A slogan is a permanent asset that sits under a logo for years and gets read several hundred times by the same person. They fail in opposite directions.

**What carries, unchanged:**
- **You are not selling the product, you are selling the feeling.** This is the whole job. 2MAN's existing line is proof: عيش جو المغامرة sells the feeling and never mentions an ice pop, which is exactly why it works and why it stays.
- **Never describe the product, never list a feature.** "أجود المشروبات بأفضل الأسعار" is a death sentence in six words.
- **The gut check: does it make me think?** If nothing stirs, it is wallpaper.
- **Fear or gain, decided before you write.** For children's food it is gain, every time. Fear sells insurance, not a jelly sweet.
- **Never lie, never exaggerate.** Doubly true on food, where a claim can also be a regulatory problem.
- **Talk like a person, never like a company.**

**What does NOT carry, and this is where people wreck it:**
- **Specific numbers and time frames.** "In 30 days, using 3 principles" is a superb headline and a dead slogan. A brand line has to sit on eighteen SKUs for three years.
- **Urgency and deadlines.** Those belong to the *campaign* line, never the brand line. The giveaway song is where they come back (see §5).
- **Long copy and skim formatting.** Irrelevant. A slogan is four to six words.
- **The "So What?" ladder** carries, but you stop differently. A headline climbs to the emotion and *states* it. A slogan climbs to the emotion and then finds the shortest way to *gesture* at it without naming it. Naming the feeling outright is what produces greeting-card copy.

## 2. Decide who is being spoken to, before any words

The single most common failure in food branding: the person who eats it and the person who pays for it are two different people, and one line tries to serve both.

| Brand | Eater | Buyer | **The slogan talks to** |
|---|---|---|---|
| BeBo | child | mother | **the mother** — she is the one deciding, and the pack's characters already handle the child |
| AlRawy | household | the person shopping for it | **the buyer**, and it is the only one of the four that is genuinely adult-facing |
| 2MAN | older kid | the kid, mostly own pocket money | **the kid** — which is why عيش جو المغامرة is pitched where it is |
| POLEKA | young child | mother, under pressure from the child | **the child**, deliberately — POLEKA's job is to be asked for by name |

Get this wrong and the line is homeless. It is not a stylistic choice, it decides the register, the vocabulary and whether the line is even in colloquial or standard Arabic.

## 3. The feeling, per brand

Run the "So What?" ladder until it stops. This is the hard part of the job and it is language-independent, so it gets done before a single word is written.

**BeBo** — one cheap sachet makes a full jug.
> So what? A whole jug of drink for almost nothing. → So what? She can say yes instead of no. → So what? The child gets the treat and she does not feel the cost.
>
> **The feeling: permission. Yes, without the wince.** BeBo's product truth is that it is cheap enough to never have to refuse.

**AlRawy** — real fruit nectar, photographed fruit, not illustration.
> So what? It is actual fruit, not flavoured sugar. → So what? She is not compromising when she puts it in a lunchbox. → So what? She did not take the lazy option.
>
> **The feeling: I did not cut a corner.** Reassurance, the same shape as the accounting-software example in [[marketing-copywriting]]. This is why AlRawy must never borrow BeBo's playfulness.

**2MAN** — ice pops, adventure, motion, cold.
> **The feeling: the day is yours, go outside.** Already solved. عيش جو المغامرة sells the feeling and not the product, which is the whole test. **It stays.** Replacing a working line to justify a deliverable destroys equity TSA was hired to build.

**POLEKA** — jelly candy, each SKU a full-bleed animal character.
> So what? Every pouch is a character, not a sweet. → So what? The child plays with it before eating it. → So what? There is a next one to want.
>
> **The feeling: it is a toy you are allowed to eat.** Play and collection, not flavour.

## 4. How the lines actually get written

1. **Voice guide first.** Slogans are Phase 2 for a reason. Without the voice guide there is no decided register, so a slogan is a guess wearing a serif font. Do not start here.
2. **Write in Arabic, out loud, first.** Never write English and translate. A translated slogan is always dead, because Egyptian colloquial lives on rhythm and wordplay that does not survive the crossing. English gets fitted afterwards, and if it will not fit, the brand carries the Arabic line alone. That is allowed and it is better than a limp English twin.
3. **Volume, then cut.** Twenty to thirty lines per brand, not three. You do not write the line, you find it among the ones you threw away.
4. **Say it to a ten year old in Alexandria.** If it needs explaining, it is finished.
5. **Present one line per brand, with at most two alternates.** Clause 1 gives TSA execution and the client the approval of an *outcome*. Handing over thirty options invites the client to design, which is the exact thing the contract keeps them out of, and it makes TSA look like it could not decide.

### The six tests a line has to pass

1. Does it sell the feeling, or describe the product? Describing is an automatic fail.
2. **Does it survive two hundred repetitions?** A joke dies on the third hearing. This is the test headlines never have to pass and slogans always do.
3. Does it still work across every SKU in the family? If it only works on mango, it is a campaign line, not a brand line.
4. **Could a competitor say the same sentence?** If yes, it is worth nothing. "Best taste, best price" is claimable by every factory in Egypt.
5. Does it sound right out loud, in the mouth of the person in §2?
6. Does it hold with nothing next to it but the logo, no pack, no photo, no context?

**And the standing rule from [[marketing-copywriting]]: no em-dashes in any copy that goes to a client or a customer.** Commas, full stops, brackets.

## 5. The giveaway song is a different animal

The scope asks for it inside the same deliverable, but it obeys campaign rules, not brand rules. **Here the direct-response levers come back on:** urgency is allowed, specificity is allowed, a deadline is allowed, because a giveaway opens and closes.

What it has to do:

- **Carry the brand name inside the hook**, not in a line before or after it. A song that can be sung without naming the product is a gift to the competition.
- **Sit on a meter that can be chanted**, because it will be performed badly by children, which is the point and the distribution.
- **Name the mechanic in the second line.** What you do to enter, in the song, or the song does no work.
- **Be written, not produced.** The scope says "written and ready for posts." Production is not in the Foundation and is not in Package A, so if the client expects a recorded track that is a clause 7 quote.

The campaign lockup (the fixed visual treatment carried across every giveaway asset) is a **Package A** line item, not a Foundation one. It gets designed once the colour and type system exists, and it is the thing that stops four brands running four different-looking giveaways.
